Enterprise Engineering Platform (EEP)

The platform had the data. Engineers still couldn’t find the answer.

I joined Enterprise Engineering Platform at Day 1 as the sole UX lead and shaped the product from an early internal tool into a broader engineering platform. The hard part was not adding more information. It was deciding what mattered to whom.

The problem

One engineering organization, too many places to look.

Service ownership, operational health, engineering performance, documentation, and governance lived across different systems. Developers needed fast answers during incidents. Engineering leaders needed a broader view. Executives needed something closer to a portfolio view.

The product risk was obvious: keep solving each request as a feature and Enterprise Engineering would become another collection of screens no one could hold in their head.

What I learned

The real unit of design was the decision, not the page.

I interviewed developers, SREs, architects, engineering directors, and DevEx teams. Across the conversations, the same pattern kept showing up: people were navigating systems to answer a small set of questions.

Is the service healthy? Who owns it? What changed?

That became the organizing principle for the experience.

The decision
Design decision

I used progressive disclosure: answer the immediate question first, then let people go deeper only when they need the detail.

That changed the information architecture. Instead of exposing the whole engineering ecosystem at once, the product surfaced the decision-critical layer and let users drill into the underlying data when required.

Service Catalog

A clearer path from a service to its owner, context, documentation, and operational status.

Engineering Scorecards

A common way to look at engineering health without forcing every team into the same workflow.

Executive Dashboard

A higher-level view for leaders who needed portfolio signal rather than operational detail.

AI-assisted capabilities

Search, recommendations, and summaries added later to the existing workflow rather than bolted on as a separate AI product.

How I worked

No handoff. The design work stayed in the room.

I worked continuously with Product, Platform Engineering, Security, Architecture, and DevEx. We used prototypes, scenario-based usability testing, and pilot deployments with real engineering teams to challenge the model before implementation.

What changed

The product became a path to an answer, not another place to search.

The clearest example was service ownership. A workflow that previously required navigating across systems dropped from roughly 12 clicks to 2 inside Enterprise Engineering.

Fidelity Design System + GenUI

Teams were moving fast by breaking the system. I changed the system instead.

At this scale, a design system is not a library sitting in Figma. It is an operating model. I focused on how teams contribute, how decisions move between design and engineering, and what happens when the interface can be generated by AI.

The problem

The central team could not be the bottleneck.

Product teams had legitimate reasons to move quickly. The central system had legitimate reasons to protect consistency. When those two goals collided, teams recreated patterns or created local solutions that were hard to bring back into the shared system.

The problem was less about missing components and more about who gets to make a design decision, how that decision becomes reusable, and who owns it afterward.

The decision
Federated contribution model

Let product teams solve real needs, then make the useful part of that solution part of the shared system.

Teams could extend the system when a product need demanded it, but the expectation was that validated improvements would come back into the shared system rather than becoming permanent forks.

That changed the relationship with the system team. The goal was no longer to approve or reject requests. It was to make contribution safe, visible, and worth doing.

[IMAGE: Federated contribution model] Show the loop: product need → extension → validation → contribution → shared system.
Design + engineering

The system had to work in the real product pipeline.

We worked around shared tokens, Figma variables, component states, usage guidance, and implementation constraints rather than treating engineering as the last step.

The outcome was a system that distributed design decisions, not just components.

The GenUI shift

Then the interface stopped being entirely manual.

Generative UI creates a different design-system problem. When interfaces can be assembled dynamically, consistency cannot depend on a designer manually picking the right components every time.

My work extended the system thinking into an AI-readable specification layer: design intent, composition rules, accessibility expectations, and interaction guidance that can inform AI-assisted product experiences.

The shift

A design system should define not only what can be built, but how an experience should be assembled.

“Ravi introduced AI-assisted approaches to our workflow that changed how we approached the problem entirely.” David McAlpine · Principal Technical Writer · AI Context Architecture & Prompt Orchestration

What I watched

Adoption mattered more than visual consistency.

I looked at whether teams could use the system to resolve repeated design problems faster and with less duplicated work. That kept the work tied to product delivery rather than turning the system into a catalog maintained for its own sake.

What changed

The system became organizational infrastructure.

The contribution model and system practices supported 14 product lines and more than 200 designers and engineers, while reducing the central component backlog and shortening recurring delivery work.

Enterprise Application Health Platform

When the dashboard failed, I redesigned the decision model.

The first version gave executives a lot of information and very little direction. When usage dropped, I treated that as evidence that the product hypothesis was wrong, not as a request for another visual refresh.

The problem

We had visibility. We did not have enough direction.

Application health data was spread across enterprise systems. Leaders were looking at security, DevOps, cloud cost, lifecycle, AI readiness, and other signals, but the experience still made them do too much interpretation themselves.

The product needed to work across 7 business units and more than 20,000 applications without turning into a giant analytics surface.

The first release

The hypothesis was reasonable. The behavior was not.

We initially designed for broad visibility and drill-down exploration. The assumption was that giving executives a complete picture would help them identify what needed attention.

After launch, executive adoption dropped to approximately 15% within three months.

“It’s beautiful, but I don’t know what I’m supposed to do.” Executive feedback that changed the direction of the product
The decision
The pivot

Stop making executives explore the data. Surface the exceptions that deserve action.

I went back to the decision problem and reframed the information architecture around exception-based decision support. Critical risk appeared first, followed by accountability and the path to action. Detailed analytics stayed available, but it moved behind the primary decision.

[IMAGE: IA shift — “explore everything” → “surface the exception”] This is the key thinking visual for the case study. Let the diagram do more work than a page of explanation.
How I worked

The adoption problem became the research signal.

I worked with Product, Engineering, Enterprise Architecture, and CIO-level stakeholders throughout the redesign. Instead of iterating blindly on the existing dashboard, the team used the adoption failure to reopen the underlying product question.

That made the design work part of the decision process itself, not just the final presentation layer.

What changed

The experience moved from passive monitoring toward operational action.

The redesign gave leaders a much faster way to identify critical risk, while creating a clearer path from signal to accountability and action.
